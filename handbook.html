Handbook

Welcome to the Handbook of Jewish AI Ethics, an annotated bibliography of key topics at the intersection of artificial intelligence and Jewish thought. Each entry lists the primary sources (Tanakh, Talmud, Midrash, codes, responsa, and later rabbinic works) and the secondary literature that bear on its topic, with a short annotation on each source and cross-references to related entries. The entries are meant to ground further scholarship and research. The full handbook, in which each entry opens with an essay surveying the sources and the questions they raise, is being prepared as a book. As always, be sure to contact us with any feedback and/or additional resources you'd like to see here.

Click on any topic below to read its bibliography, or use the search bar (top right) to find specific terms across all entries.

Primary Sources

  • Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 34b; Gittin 29b; Kiddushin 41b-43a; Bava Metzia 10a-11b, 96a; Nedarim 72b. Establishes the attribution formula, tests who can serve as an agent, and distinguishes between an agent as the principal's extension and an independently empowered actor.

  • Ketzot HaḤoshen, simanim 182 and 188; Sefer ha-Mikneh 15:4-8. (Aryeh Leib Heller). Discussions of the nature of agency, including whether the agent acts as an extension of the principal or through independently delegated power.

  • Sefer ha-Mikneh, vol 2, no. 23. (Eliezer Zussman Sofer). Extensive discussion covering the talmudic evidence and applications of various forms of agency.

Secondary Sources

  • Ariel, Yaakov. "Binah Melakhutit ke-'Yada Arikhta'" ["Artificial Intelligence as an 'Extended Hand'"; Hebrew]. Teḥumin 46 (5786/2026): 105-110. Analyzes whether AI-mediated acts are attributable to the operator as direct action, indirect causation, or another halakhic category, with applications to damages and Shabbat.

  • Klein, David. "Manganon Ha-Sheliḥut" ["The Mechanism of Agency"; Hebrew]. Yarḥon Ha-Otzar 105 (Av 5785/2025): 449-464. Examines the conceptual underpinnings of sheliḥut in rabbinic literature, including an AI-enabled artificial limb as an analogy for the "extended hand" model.

  • Kalman, David Zvi. "Artificial Intelligence and Jewish Thought." In The Cambridge Companion to Religion and Artificial Intelligence, edited by Beth Singler and Fraser Watts, 69-87. Cambridge University Press, 2024. Discusses sheliḥut, animal-liability, and grama as possible but underdetermined frameworks for AI.

  • Gabriel, Iason, et al. "The Ethics of Advanced AI Assistants." arXiv (2024). Maps the ethical risks created when AI assistants plan and perform sequences of actions on users' behalf, including value alignment, user autonomy, misuse, and effects on non-users.

  • National Institute of Standards and Technology. Accelerating the Adoption of Software and AI Agent Identity and Authorization: Concept Paper. February 2026. Proposes identity, authorization, access-delegation, logging, and provenance controls that preserve accountability when software agents act for human users or organizations.

Primary Sources

  • Genesis 1:27. The creation of humanity be-tselem Elohim (in the divine image), establishing the foundational equality and dignity of all persons regardless of social category.

  • Exodus 23:3. "Do not favor the poor in his cause."

  • Exodus 23:6. "Do not pervert the judgment of the poor in his cause."

  • Leviticus 19:15. "You shall not render an unfair decision; do not favor the poor or show deference to the rich; judge your kinsman fairly."

  • Deuteronomy 10:18-19. The obligation to love and provide for the stranger (ger), grounded in Israel's experience of vulnerability in Egypt.

  • Isaiah 11:4. The Messiah "will judge the poor justly and decide with equity for the meek of the earth." Messianic justice attends to the vulnerable.

  • Isaiah 56:3-7. Reference to members of other nations.

  • Amos 9:7. Indication that God's providence extends to non-Israelites.

  • Malachi 2:10. "Have we not all one Father? Did not one God create us?"

  • Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5. Adam was created alone "for the sake of peace among people, so that no one could say to another, 'My father was greater than yours.'" Also teaches that whoever destroys a single life is as if they destroyed an entire world.

  • Pirkei Avot 1:6. Joshua ben Perahiah's teaching to "judge every person favorably."

  • Pirkei Avot 4:3. "Do not despise any person, and do not discriminate against anything, for there is no person who does not have their hour, and no thing that does not have its place."

  • Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 31a. Hillel's formulation of the Golden Rule: "That which is hateful to you, do not do to another."

  • Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 54b. Responsibility to protest wrongdoing at the level of household, town, and world.

  • Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 10a. Berurya's teaching to pray for the end of sin rather than the destruction of sinners; may suggest focus on reforming systems rather than punishing individuals.

  • Babylonian Talmud, Bava Metzia 83a. The case of Rabbah bar Rav Huna and the porters; Rav rules that justice requires attending to the workers' poverty, not merely applying the formal law.

  • Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 37a. Extended discussion of human dignity and the infinite value of each individual life.

  • Babylonian Talmud, Shevuot 31a. Procedural requirements ensuring that litigants appear equal before the court; wealthy parties may not dress more impressively than poor ones.

  • Rashbatz (R. Shimon ben Tzemach Duran), Tashbetz 1:158; cf. 1:161; Maharik shoresh 66; Radbaz 5:2248. The enactments and statutes of kings are general rather than particular; a directive aimed at a single individual does not carry the force of law.

  • Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Hilkhot Matanot Aniyim 7:3. The obligation to provide for the poor according to their individual needs, preserving dignity through attention to particularity.

Secondary Sources

Modern Jewish Theology

  • Buber, Martin. I and Thou. Translated by Walter Kaufmann. New York: Scribner, 1970 [orig. Ich und Du, 1923]. Distinguishes the I-Thou relation of direct encounter from the I-It relation in which another is treated as an object of description and use.

  • Levinas, Emmanuel. Totality and Infinity: An Essay on Exteriority. Translated by Alphonso Lingis. Pittsburgh: Duquesne University Press, 1969 [orig. Totalité et Infini, 1961]. Argues that the face of the other makes an ethical claim prior to any category applied to them, and criticizes the reduction of persons to instances within a totality of general concepts.

  • Hughes, Aaron W. Rethinking Jewish Philosophy: Beyond Particularism and Universalism. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2014. Critically examines the tension between particularist and universalist strands in Jewish thought; useful for contextualizing claims about Judaism's stance on human equality without oversimplifying the tradition.

  • Sacks, Jonathan. The Dignity of Difference: How to Avoid the Clash of Civilizations. London: Continuum, 2002. Argues that human dignity requires recognition of difference rather than homogenization; relevant to debates about whether systems should be "colorblind" or attend to group membership.

  • Feit, Joseph. "The Algorithm That Couldn't See: AI Ethics and the Halakhic Discipline of Perception." The Lehrhaus, March 23, 2026. Argues that because AI systems perceive through statistical patterns, using them as a stand-in for human judgement erodes our moral sensitivity.

Jewish Ethics, Universalism, and Human Dignity

  • Novak, David. The Image of the Non-Jew in Judaism: An Historical and Constructive Study of the Noahide Laws. New York: Edwin Mellen Press, 1983. The classic treatment of how halakhah addresses the moral and legal status of gentiles; argues that the Noahide laws establish a framework of universal human dignity grounded in creation.

  • Soloveichik, Aharon. "Civil Rights and the Dignity of Man." In Logic of the Heart, Logic of the Mind: Wisdom and Reflections on Topics of Our Times, 61-70. Jerusalem: Genesis Jerusalem Press, 1991. A forceful Orthodox rabbinic argument for racial equality grounded in tselem Elohim; written in the context of the American civil rights movement, it demonstrates how classical Jewish sources can be marshaled against discrimination.

  • Dorff, Elliot N. To Do the Right and the Good: A Jewish Approach to Modern Social Ethics. Philadelphia: Jewish Publication Society, 2002. Accessible treatment of Jewish social ethics including justice, equality, and communal responsibility; includes discussion of affirmative action and systemic remedies.

  • Rosenberg, Shalom. "On the Principles of Jewish Ethics: 'Love Your Neighbor as Yourself'—Universalism and Equality" [Hebrew]. Ma'aseh Ḥoshev 2. Koren, 2018, 39-52. Develops equality, universalizability, and the image of God as related but nonidentical moral principles; included in the local source collection.

  • Navon, Haim. "I Am a Creature and My Fellow Is a Creature: The Value of Equality—A Jewish View" [Hebrew]. Ma'aseh Ḥoshev 2. Koren, 2018, 53-70. Argues that equality affirms common human standing without requiring identical treatment of every relevant difference; included in the local source collection.

Algorithmic Bias and Fairness

  • National Institute of Standards and Technology. Towards a Standard for Identifying and Managing Bias in Artificial Intelligence. NIST Special Publication 1270. March 2022. Available online. Distinguishes systemic, statistical and computational, and human bias, and emphasizes that harmful outcomes can arise without discriminatory intent.

  • Barocas, Solon, and Andrew D. Selbst. "Big Data's Disparate Impact." California Law Review 104, no. 3 (2016): 671-732. Foundational legal analysis demonstrating how data mining can reproduce patterns of discrimination even without discriminatory intent; applies disparate impact doctrine to algorithmic decision-making.

  • Hardt, Moritz. "How Big Data Is Unfair." Medium, September 26, 2014. Accessible introduction to the concept of unfairness in machine learning, explaining how predictive models can systematically disadvantage certain groups even when built on accurate statistical correlations.

  • Crawford, Kate. "The Hidden Biases in Big Data." Harvard Business Review, 2013. Explains how biases enter data systems through collection methods, sampling, and the assumptions embedded in the design process, often invisibly.

  • Hao, Karen. "This Is How AI Bias Really Happens—and Why It's so Hard to Fix." MIT Technology Review, February 4, 2019. Journalistic overview identifying three key stages at which bias enters AI systems: problem framing, data collection, and data preparation; valuable for its clarity in explaining technical dynamics to non-specialist audiences.

  • Helbing, Dirk. "Will Democracy Survive Big Data and Artificial Intelligence?" Scientific American, February 25, 2017. Warns that scoring and steering citizens at scale converts ostensibly neutral optimization into political control.

  • Johnson, Gabbrielle M. "Algorithmic Bias: On the Implicit Biases of Social Technology." Synthese 198 (2020): 9941-61. Philosophical analysis drawing on research in implicit bias to argue that algorithmic systems can encode the same kinds of unconscious prejudices found in human cognition, complicating claims that automation eliminates bias.

  • Kleinberg, Jon, Himabindu Lakkaraju, Jure Leskovec, Jens Ludwig, and Sendhil Mullainathan. "Human Decisions and Machine Predictions." Quarterly Journal of Economics 133, no. 1 (2018a): 237-293. Empirical study of bail decisions finding that machine predictions could reduce jailing and crime rates simultaneously relative to human judges.

  • Kleinberg, Jon, Jens Ludwig, Sendhil Mullainathan, and Cass R. Sunstein. "Discrimination in the Age of Algorithms." Journal of Legal Analysis 10 (2018b): 113-174. Argues that algorithms make discrimination more detectable and provable than human decision-making, which resists inspection.

  • Lepri, Bruno, Nuria Oliver, Emmanuel Letouzé, Alex Pentland, and Patrick Vinck. "Fair, Transparent, and Accountable Algorithmic Decision-Making Processes." Philosophy & Technology 31, no. 4 (2017): 611-27. Proposes a framework for ensuring that algorithmic decision-making processes meet standards of fairness, transparency, and accountability; the emphasis on procedural safeguards resonates with the Talmudic concern for controlling information that reaches the judge.

  • Spaulding, Norman W. "Is Human Judgment Necessary? Artificial Intelligence, Algorithmic Governance, and the Law." In The Oxford Handbook of Ethics of AI, edited by Markus D. Dubber, Frank Pasquale, and Sunit Das. New York: Oxford University Press, 2020. Explores the ethical implications of automating adjudication generally.

  • Stewart, Matthew. "Programming Fairness in Algorithms." Towards Data Science, July 2, 2020. Practical walk-through of the competing formal fairness criteria and why satisfying several at once is generally impossible.

  • Xiang, Alice. "Reconciling Legal and Technical Approaches to Algorithmic Bias." Tennessee Law Review 88, no. 3 (2021). Examines the gap between legal definitions of discrimination and technical definitions of fairness in machine learning; argues that bridging this gap requires attention to context and purpose, echoing the halakhic insistence that justice cannot be reduced to formal rules alone.

  • Schauer, Frederick. Profiles, Probabilities, and Stereotypes. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 2003. The foundational treatment of when it is defensible to judge a person by a generalization drawn from a group to which they belong; predates the algorithmic debate but frames it.

  • Eidelson, Benjamin. Discrimination and Disrespect. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2015. Grounds the duty to treat people as individuals in respect for autonomy, arguing that a decision-maker must attend to how a person has exercised agency to distinguish themselves from group patterns. See also his "Respect, Individualism, and Colorblindness," Yale Law Journal 129 (2020).

  • Lippert-Rasmussen, Kasper. "'We Are All Different': Statistical Discrimination and the Right to Be Treated as an Individual." The Journal of Ethics 15, nos. 1-2 (2011). Argues that no coherent right to be treated as an individual can be formulated, since all practical judgment proceeds by generalization; the principal statement of the opposing position.

  • Hellman, Deborah. "Algorithmic Fairness." The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, July 30, 2025. Survey of the field from an ethical-philosophical perspective.

  • Binns, Reuben. "Human Judgment in Algorithmic Loops: Individual Justice and Automated Decision-Making." Regulation & Governance 16, no. 1 (2022). Examines whether inserting human review into automated decisions delivers the individualized justice its advocates claim.

  • Barocas, Solon, Moritz Hardt, and Arvind Narayanan. Fairness and Machine Learning: Limitations and Opportunities. MIT Press, 2023. Technical but accessible treatment of fairness in machine learning; explains the mathematical trade-offs between different fairness definitions and why they cannot all be satisfied simultaneously.

Jewish Legal Safeguards Against Bias

  • Lichtenstein, Aharon. "The Human and Social Factor in Halakha." Tradition 36, no. 1 (2002): 1-25. Explores when human dignity (kevod ha-beriyot) overrides other halakhic considerations; foundational for understanding how dignity claims function in Jewish law and how they might inform the design of systems that affect human welfare.

  • Greenberg, Yitzhak. "Justice, Justice Shall You Pursue." American Jewish University, 2021. Analysis of Parashat Shoftim arguing that Jewish tradition supports affirmative action to remedy systemic injustice while warning against abandoning individual judgment; directly applicable to debates about how to balance categorical remedies with case-by-case assessment.

  • Sadan, Uri. "Akhifa Bereranit ba-Halakha" [Selective Enforcement in Halakhah; Hebrew]. Teḥumin 38 (5778/2018): 345-354. Asks whether an authority may lawfully enforce a rule against some violators and not others, distinguishing discrimination against an individual from discrimination against a group; relevant to predictive policing and to any system that concentrates enforcement on a subset of a population.

  • Sadan, Uri. "Ha'adafa Metakenet le-Or ha-Halakha" [Affirmative Action in Light of Halakhah; Hebrew]. Teḥumin 42 (5782/2022): 207-221. Distinguishes formal from substantive equality and opportunity from outcome, and asks whether halakhah permits or requires preferential treatment to remedy the underrepresentation of groups, in public service and in private hiring.

  • Bar-Ilan, Naftali. "Ekronot Shivyon" [Principles of Equality; Hebrew]. In Mishtar u-Medina be-Yisrael al pi ha-Torah [Government and State in Israel According to the Torah], vol. 4, ch. 64, 1530-1540. Eretz Hemdah, 5773/2013. Treats equality as a constraint on the institutions of government, including the king, the high court, and communal officers, drawing extensively on medieval and early modern communal record books. Concludes that these principles governing the relation between the individual and the authorities remain in force today.

Applied AI Ethics and Discrimination

  • Anti-Defamation League (ADL). Generating Hate: Anti-Jewish and Anti-Israel Bias in Leading Large Language Models. March 20, 2025. Evaluates GPT, Claude, Gemini, and Llama and reports variation in measured anti-Jewish and anti-Israel bias.

  • Anti-Defamation League. ADL AI Index. January 2026. Evaluates six models across more than 25,000 interactions and 37 topical subcategories, while emphasizing that the results are a snapshot of systems that continue to change.

  • O'Neil, Cathy. Weapons of Math Destruction: How Big Data Increases Inequality and Threatens Democracy. New York: Crown, 2016. Essential account of how automated systems encode and amplify discrimination by treating individuals as instances of statistical reference classes; documents harms in hiring, lending, criminal justice, and education.

  • Christian, Brian. The Alignment Problem: Machine Learning and Human Values. New York: W. W. Norton, 2020. Explains how systems trained on biased data perpetuate those biases and the technical challenges of ensuring AI acts in accordance with human values.

  • Harcourt, Bernard E. Against Prediction: Profiling, Policing, and Punishing in an Actuarial Age. Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 2007. Book-length critique of actuarial prediction in the criminal justice system, predating much of the current discourse on algorithmic or AI systems.

  • Lum, Kristian, and William Isaac. "To Predict and Serve?" Significance 13, no. 5 (2016): 14-19. Demonstrates through analysis of drug-related arrest data that predictive policing algorithms trained on historically biased police data reproduce and amplify those biases, directing disproportionate enforcement to minority communities.

  • Corbett-Davies, Sam, Emma Pierson, Avi Feller, and Sharad Goel. "A Computer Program Used for Bail and Sentencing Decisions Was Labeled Biased against Blacks. It's Actually Not That Clear." The Washington Post, October 17, 2016. Nuanced analysis of the COMPAS recidivism algorithm controversy, illustrating that different mathematically valid definitions of fairness can yield contradictory assessments of the same system—a finding with implications for the halakhic principle that justice requires attention to context rather than mechanical application of rules.

Primary Sources

  • Leviticus 25:14-17. Prohibits wronging another in commerce and in speech. The later legal tradition distinguishes monetary ona'ah from verbal wronging.

  • Deuteronomy 1:17. Commands judges to "hear the small and the great alike."

  • Mishnah Bava Metzia 4:3-12; Babylonian Talmud, Bava Metzia 49b-58b. Develops the thresholds, remedies, timing rules, and exclusions of ona'ah.

  • Tosefta Bava Metzia 11:23; Babylonian Talmud, Bava Batra 8b-9a. Authorizes townspeople to establish rules for prices, measures, and wages and to enforce their agreement, subject in the Talmudic discussion to legitimate communal authority.

  • Babylonian Talmud, Bava Batra 89a-91a. Addresses accurate measures, market supervision, hoarding, and profiteering in necessities.

  • Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Laws of Sale 12-14; Shulḥan Arukh, Ḥoshen Mishpat 227; 231. Codifies ona'ah, its remedies and exceptions, and public regulation of profit margins on necessities.

Secondary Sources

Jewish Law and Ethics

  • Rechnitz, Ido. "Defining the Market Price Today" [Hebrew]. Teḥumin 46 (5786/2026): 215-226. Uses contemporary online price data to discuss transaction terms, median prices, and whether modern goods have a determinate market price.

  • Warhaftig, Itamar. "Consumer Protection: Price and Wage Levels" [Hebrew]. Teḥumin 1 (5740/1980): 444-490. Surveys market-price formation, profit limitations, essential goods, price fixing, and consumer vulnerability. An English version was published in Crossroads: Halacha and the Modern World, vol. 1.

  • Navon, Haim. "On the Torah's Economic Conception" [Hebrew]. Tzohar 12 (5763/2003): 97-107. Examines the balance among free competition, equality, consumer welfare, economic efficiency, and public supervision.

  • Navon, Haim. "I Am a Creature and My Fellow Is a Creature: The Value of Equality—A Jewish View" [Hebrew]. Ma'aseh Ḥoshev 2. Koren, 2018, 53-70. Argues that Jewish equality affirms common human standing without requiring identical treatment of every relevant difference.

  • Weiss, Asher Zelig. "The Law of Ona'ah Today" [Hebrew]. In Mishpetei Eretz III: Property and Commerce, 342-345. Ofra: Mishpetei Eretz Institute, 5770/2010. Argues that many modern products lack one determinate market price, while retaining ona'ah for exceptional markets with a fixed or regulated price.

  • Goldberg, Zalman Nehemiah. "The Law of Ona'ah Today" [Hebrew]. In Mishpetei Eretz III: Property and Commerce, 335-341. Ofra: Mishpetei Eretz Institute, 5770/2010. Maintains that a market price can be identified in a market with varied prices and generally looks to the price charged by most stores.

  • Kleiman, Ephraim. "'Just Price' in Talmudic Literature." History of Political Economy 19, no. 1 (1987): 23-45. Interprets ona'ah primarily as protection of market transactions rather than a comprehensive system of price control.

Contemporary Pricing Research and Policy

  • Bar-Gill, Oren. "Algorithmic Price Discrimination When Demand Is a Function of Both Preferences and (Mis)perceptions." University of Chicago Law Review 86, no. 2 (2019). Distinguishes pricing based on preferences from pricing that exploits demand-inflating consumer misperceptions.

  • Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development. Personalised Pricing in the Digital Era. OECD Roundtables on Competition Policy Papers 222 (2018). Defines personalized pricing, distinguishes it from adjacent practices, and reviews competition and consumer-welfare concerns.

  • U.S. Department of Justice, Antitrust Division. U.S. and Plaintiff States v. RealPage, Inc. Case docket and filings, updated July 6, 2026. Current enforcement record concerning alleged algorithmic coordination and use of competitors' nonpublic rental data.

Secondary Sources

The Alignment Problem in AI

  • Yudkowsky, Eliezer, and Nate Soares. If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies: Why Superhuman AI Would Kill Us All. New York: Little, Brown and Company, 2025. A forceful statement of the authors' argument that building superhuman AI without a solution to alignment would create an extinction risk.

  • Bostrom, Nick. Superintelligence: Paths, Dangers, Strategies. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2014. An influential statement of existential risk from advanced AI, including the "control problem" of maintaining human authority over superintelligent systems.

  • Christian, Brian. The Alignment Problem: Machine Learning and Human Values. New York: W. W. Norton, 2020. An accessible book-length account of alignment problems through the history and contemporary practice of machine learning.

  • Dafoe, Allan. AI Governance: A Research Agenda. Centre for the Governance of AI, 2018. Proposes a framework and research agenda for the institutional and political dimensions of AI governance.

  • International AI Safety Report 2026. Annual report, February 3, 2026. Sec. 2.2.2 reviews the capabilities, propensities, and deployment conditions that would be required for severe loss-of-control scenarios, while emphasizing uncertainty and disagreement about their likelihood.

Golem Traditions and the Control Problem

  • Charpa, Ulrich. "Synthetic Biology and the Golem of Prague: Philosophical Reflections on a Suggestive Metaphor." Perspectives in Biology and Medicine 55, no. 4 (2012): 554-70. Examines the golem as a metaphor for synthetic biology and argues that the comparison can obscure important ontological, practical, and ethical differences.

  • Idel, Moshe. Golem: Jewish Magical and Mystical Traditions on the Artificial Anthropoid. Albany: State University of New York Press, 1990. A comprehensive historical study of ancient, medieval, early-modern, and modern golem traditions, including the Golem of Chełm and halakhic discussions.

  • Scholem, Gershom. "The Idea of the Golem." In On the Kabbalah and Its Symbolism, 158-204. New York: Schocken Books, 1965. A classic study of the religious and symbolic history of the golem.

Jewish Demonology and Agent Reliability

  • Ronis, Sara. Demons in the Details: Demonic Discourse and Rabbinic Culture in Late Antique Babylonia. University of California Press, 2022. The most comprehensive recent study of Babylonian Talmudic demonology.

  • Bohak, Gideon. Ancient Jewish Magic: A History. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2008. Survey of Jewish magical practices including demon adjuration.

AI and Contemporary Jewish Ethics

  • Grossman, Yitzhak. "Jewish Perspectives on Artificial Intelligence and Synthetic Biology." Ḥakirah 35 (2024): 61-93. Surveys rabbinic sources on artificial creation, including R. Tsadok ha-Kohen's warning that a created being might become harmful if allowed to grow.

Primary Sources

  • Exodus 20:12; Deuteronomy 5:16. The fifth commandment: "Honor your father and your mother."

  • Leviticus 19:3. "A man shall fear his mother and his father." Rashi notes the inversion of the usual order (father before mother in Ex. 20:12) and derives from this that each parent commands equal, though differently expressed, forms of reverence.

  • Babylonian Talmud, Kiddushin 31a-b. The principal talmudic discussion of kibbud av va-em, including the story of Dama ben Netina, the distinction between kibbud and mora, and extended discussion of filial service. The narrative of Rav Assi and his elderly mother (31b) is the key test case for the limits of the obligation: Rav Assi departed when his mother's demands became impossible, heard she had followed him and died, and declared "had I known, I would not have left."

  • Rashash (R. Shmuel Strashun), Hagahot ha-Rashash, on Kiddushin 31b. Explains that Rav Assi's regret was that the hardship of his departure or his very abandonment caused his mother's death; argues that Rav Assi effectively retracted his decision to leave when he sought to go and meet her, and that the Ra'avad's ruling against unilateral departure derives from this story.

  • Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Hilkhot Mamrim 6:1-3, 6:10. Codification of filial obligations; 6:10 permits delegation of care (yitzveh aḥerim lenahaigam kara'ui lahem) under duress when a parent's mental deterioration makes personal care unbearable.

  • Ra'avad (R. Abraham b. David of Posquières), Hassagot, on Hilkhot Mamrim 6:10. Dissents from Maimonides: "This is not a correct ruling. If he goes and leaves him, to whom will he give instructions to watch over him?" The Ra'avad holds that the child cannot simply depart; delegating care is only permissible if adequate care is actually secured.

  • Sukkah 49b. Distinguishes ḥesed from tzedakah, the most relevant distinction being that ḥesed requires personal investment (be-gufo).

  • Shabbat 119a; Shulḥan Arukh, Oraḥ Ḥayyim 250:1. Require personal participation in Sabbath preparations even when others can perform the work.

  • Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Hilkhot Talmud Torah 1:1. The father's personal obligation to teach his son Torah, falling first on the father himself and only secondarily on a hired teacher.

  • Sefer ha-Ḥinukh, mitzvah 33. On honoring father and mother: a person should recognize and repay the good done to him by those who brought him into the world, "and when he fixes this trait in his soul he will rise from it to recognize the goodness of God." The obligation is framed as the training of gratitude in the child, which bears on whether a discharged duty and a personally discharged duty are the same thing.

  • Sefer ha-Ḥinukh, mitzvah 66. Grounds the commandments of lending and support in God's wish that His creatures be "trained and habituated in the trait of kindness and mercy," and notes that God could provide for the poor without us; we are made agents of the giving for our own sake.

  • Shulḥan Arukh, Even ha-Ezer 71:1. Codifies the father's obligation to support sons and daughters while young; thereafter the court publicly pressures him to fulfill this duty.

  • Nahmanides on Deuteronomy 22:6. Nahmanides interprets the commandment to send away the mother bird as cultivating the trait of compassion in the one who performs it—the principle that mitzvot shape moral character, not merely produce outcomes.

  • Genesis 2:15. Le-ovdah u-le-shomrah: humanity is placed in Eden "to work it and to guard it" before the curse of toil. Targum Yonatan, Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer ch. 12, Bereshit Rabbah 16:5, and Yalkut Shimoni (Bereshit §20) read the verse as referring to spiritual rather than physical labor.

  • Leviticus 19:9-10. The laws of pe'ah, leket, and peret: landowners are commanded to leave portions of their produce for the poor and the stranger, building generosity into the productive process itself.

  • Leviticus 25:1-55. The legislation of shemittah (sabbatical year) and yovel (Jubilee): periodic agricultural rest, restoration of ancestral landholdings, and the release of Israelite indentured servants.

  • Deuteronomy 15:1-11. The periodic remission of debts, followed by the command, "You shall not harden your heart or close your hand against your needy brother." The chapter pairs an obligation to lend and give with the realistic concession that "the poor will never cease from the land."

  • Deuteronomy 24:14-15. Requires timely payment of wages, framing labor as a relation of mutual obligation rather than purely economic exchange.

  • Mishnah Ketubot 5:5. Even a woman who brings many servants into her marriage must still perform some labor. R. Eliezer says idleness leads to licentiousness, while Rabban Shimon ben Gamliel says it leads to shi'amum (boredom or mental deterioration).

  • Mishnah Sanhedrin 3:3 (with Maimonides' commentary). Primary Sources

  • Jeremiah and Ben Sira (MS Vat. 299). A speaking golem in the tradition erases the alef on its forehead (turning emet, truth, into met, dead) and admonishes its makers to study the powers of creation but not to use them.

  • Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 38b. Describes Adam's first hours as a golem: an unformed body before the divine infusion of soul.

  • Sanhedrin 65b. The locus classicus for artificial creation. Rava creates a man and sends him to R. Zeira, who finds the creature mute and orders it to return to dust. Rav Ḥanina and Rav Oshaya use Sefer Yetsirah to create a calf each Sabbath eve.

  • Sanhedrin 67b. Distinguishes creation through Sefer Yetsirah (treated as legitimate use of natural knowledge) from prohibited sorcery (kishshuf), removing the halakhic obstacle to the practice.

  • Bereshit Rabbah 39:14. "Were all the dwellers of the world to come together, they could not create a single gnat and put in it a soul." A midrashic statement of the limit on human creative capacity.

  • Sefer Yetsirah 2:5-6. The ancient cosmogonic text describing the (original, divine) formation of reality through permutation of the twenty-two Hebrew letters.

  • R. Eleazar of Worms, Commentary on Sefer Yetsirah, late 12th-early 13th century. Contains medieval instructions for golem creation.

  • R. Menaḥem Meiri, Commentary on Sanhedrin 67b. Meiri reads Sefer Yetsirah creation as natural-technological rather than magical, saying that "Anything that is performed via natural action... as it is known in books of natural science that this is not impossible, it is permitted to do so."

  • R. Moshe Cordovero, Pardes Rimmonim 24:10. Kabbalistic analysis treating the golem's vitality as comparable to animal life only, denying it the higher levels of soul.

  • R. Tsvi Ashkenazi, Ḥakham Tsvi, responsum no. 93. Records the tradition of his ancestor R. Eliyahu Ba'al Shem of Chełm, who created a golem and later destroyed it for fear it would "destroy the world." Rules that such a creature cannot be counted for a minyan for want of a divinely bestowed soul.

  • R. Yaakov Emden, She'eilat Ya'avets, vol. 2, siman 82. Compares the golem's capacities to those of a trained parrot or dog.

  • R. Ḥayyim Yosef David Azulai (the Ḥida), Marit ha-Ayin on Sanhedrin 65b; Birkei Yosef OḤ 55; Mahazik Berakha 45:1. Rules that a golem cannot be counted toward a minyan and raises the concern that such creations may be unsafe.

  • R. Gershon Henoch Leiner of Radzin, Sidrei Taharot on Ohalot 5. Addresses the golem in relation to ritual impurity, personhood, the prohibition of murder, and the theoretical possibility of a fully human artificial creation.

  • R. Tsadok ha-Kohen of Lublin, Divrei Ḥalomot 6. Argues that even if a golem could be said to possess a soul, it would not be obligated in the commandments and would lack the spiritual reward-and-punishment system that grounds covenantal participation.

  • Ḥazon Ish (R. Avraham Yeshayah Karelitz), Yoreh De'ah, beginning of siman 116 (p. 66). Rules that a Sefer Yetsirah creation "has none of the rights of a Jew, and likely none of the rights of Man."

Secondary Sources

Foundational Scholarly Studies

  • Scholem, Gershom. "The Idea of the Golem." In On the Kabbalah and Its Symbolism, 158-204. New York: Schocken Books, 1965. Traces the golem from Talmudic origins through medieval Ashkenaz to modern literature.

  • Idel, Moshe. Golem: Jewish Magical and Mystical Traditions on the Artificial Anthropoid. Albany: SUNY Press, 1990. Surveys the development of the concept through Ḥasidei Ashkenaz, the Abulafian school, the Italian Renaissance, and the Safed kabbalists, and examines golem-creation as a marker of mystical accomplishment.

Historical and Cultural Studies

  • Kieval, Hillel J. "Pursuing the Golem of Prague: Jewish Culture and the Invention of a Tradition." Modern Judaism 17, no. 1 (1997): 1-23. Demonstrates that the Maharal-golem legend is a nineteenth-century invention and traces its propagation.

  • Schäfer, Peter. "The Magic of the Golem: The Early Development of the Golem Legend." Journal of Jewish Studies 46, nos. 1-2 (1995): 249-261. Analyzes the earliest stages of the tradition and the transition from talmudic speculation to medieval magical practice.

  • Gelbin, Cathy S. The Golem Returns: From German Romantic Literature to Global Jewish Culture, 1808-2008. Ann Arbor: University of Michigan Press, 2011. Literary and sociocultural study tracing modern interest in golems to nineteenth-century German Christians and reading the golem as a site of negotiation over Jewish identity.

  • Mayor, Adrienne. Gods and Robots: Myths, Machines, and Ancient Dreams of Technology. Princeton: Princeton University Press, 2018. Comparative study of artificial life in ancient mythology that situates the golem within a broader cross-cultural history of imagined automata.

Halakhic and Theological Status of Artificial Beings

  • Loike, John D., and Moshe D. Tendler. "Ma Adam Va-Teda'ehu: Halakhic Criteria for Defining Human Beings." Tradition 37, no. 2 (2003): 1-19. Develops halakhic criteria for distinguishing humans from other entities, drawing also on golem sources.

  • Bleich, J. David, and Arthur J. Jacobson. "Cloning." In Jewish Law and Contemporary Issues, 357-396. Cambridge University Press, 2015. Discusses golem literature as a halakhic precedent for cloning and the status of artificially created human beings.

  • Grossman, Yitzhak. "Jewish Perspectives on Artificial Intelligence and Synthetic Biology." Ḥakirah 35 (2024): 61-93. Halakhic survey organized around Sanhedrin 65b, including four positions on the ensoulment of artificially created beings.

  • Navon, Mois. "'Let Us Make Man in Our Image,' a Jewish Ethical Perspective on Creating Conscious Robots." AI and Ethics 4 (2024): 1235-1250. Surveys Jewish sources on creating humanoids and golems and argues against creating conscious artificial beings.

  • Rosenfeld, Azriel. "Religion and the Robot." Tradition 8, no. 3 (Fall 1966): 15-26. A pioneering treatment of whether an intelligent artificial being could hold religious status, seek conversion, or be counted as a person in halakhah; left deliberately unresolved.

  • Rosenfeld, Azriel. "Human Identity: Halakhic Issues." Tradition 16, no. 3 (1977): 58-74. Surveys competing halakhic criteria for identifying a human being—descent, form, and rational speech—and applies them to artificial and radically altered beings.

Applying the Golem to Artificial Intelligence

  • Wiener, Norbert. God & Golem, Inc.: A Comment on Certain Points Where Cybernetics Impinges on Religion. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press, 1964. An early treatment of the social and ethical implications of cybernetics; despite its title, it contains almost no discussion of Jewish tradition.

  • Scholem, Gershom. "The Golem of Prague and the Golem of Rehovoth." Commentary 41, no. 1 (January 1966): 62-65. Dedicatory address for the Weizmann Institute's "Golem Aleph" computer, delivered June 17, 1965, comparing the kabbalistic golem and the digital computer.

  • Sherwin, Byron L. Golems Among Us: How a Jewish Legend Can Help Us Navigate the Biotech Century. Chicago: Ivan R. Dee, 2004. Applies the golem tradition to the moral responsibilities of human creative power in biotechnology, cloning, and AI.

  • Rappaport, Zvi H. "Robotics and Artificial Intelligence: Jewish Ethical Perspectives." Acta Neurochirurgica Supplement 98 (2006): 9-12. Discusses ethical principles for AI and robotics through the golem legend.

  • Kalman, David Zvi. "Artificial Intelligence and Jewish Thought." In The Cambridge Companion to Religion and Artificial Intelligence, edited by Beth Singler and Fraser Watts, 69-87. Cambridge University Press, 2024. Surveys Jewish engagement with artificial humanoid beings from medieval golem literature to contemporary AI.

  • Goltz, Nachshon (Sean), John Zeleznikow, and Tracey Dowdeswell. "From the Tree of Knowledge and the Golem of Prague to Kosher Autonomous Cars." Oxford Journal of Law and Religion 9, no. 1 (2020): 132-156. Discusses AI ethics through Jewish sources, including the golem tradition.

  • Rosen, Michael. Like Silicon from Clay: What Ancient Jewish Wisdom Can Teach Us About AI. Washington, DC: AEI Press, 2025. Applies golem traditions and halakhic reasoning to contemporary AI policy debates, especially questions of liability, foreseeable harm, and the duty to prevent destructive outcomes.

  • Rubin, Charles T. "The Golem and the Limits of Artifice: On What the Jewish Legend Can (and Cannot) Teach Us About Bioethics." The New Atlantis 39 (2013): 71-83. Critical essay arguing that the contemporary use of the golem as a bioethical exemplar typically draws on the nineteenth- and twentieth-century literary versions (Rosenberg, Bloch, Leivick, Wegener) rather than the classical sources, and warning against extracting easy moral lessons from a tradition whose dramatic-loss-of-control framing is mostly modern.

  • Vudka, Amir. "The Golem in the Age of Artificial Intelligence." NECSUS: European Journal of Media Studies 9, no. 1 (2020): 101-123. Reads cinematic representations of AI through the golem narrative and argues that the figure sets the basic terms for depicting human-machine relations.
